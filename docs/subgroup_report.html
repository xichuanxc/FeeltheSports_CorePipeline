2What “the result” means here

Each participant gets two scores, each the mean of three items on the same 1–5 scale. System quality is noticeable + fitted + synchronised, and asks whether the detector worked: overall 4.39 (sd 0.72). Experience is enjoyed + engaging + would-install, and asks what they made of it: overall 3.52 (sd 1.27). Every comparison below is against these two.

The one result, and what it is not

A hypothesis from last week, tested this week

Predicted at n = 20
Tested at n = 22
Smallest cell n = 1

Last week’s note called this “the hypothesis worth testing, not claiming”. Two participants then arrived — one of them the study’s first regular player — and it held. That sequence matters: the prediction was on paper before the data that tests it.

The gradient, and how hard it is to break

Treating playing frequency as an ordered exposure gives Spearman rho = −0.533, p = 0.011 against the system-quality composite. Dropping either extreme participant leaves it significant:

all 22rho −0.533p 0.011
drop P23 (the dissenter)rho −0.484p 0.026
drop P12 (the regular player)rho −0.464p 0.034
18 unflagged sheets onlyrho −0.516p 0.028
drop both extremesrho −0.392p 0.087

Last week the same contrast rested almost entirely on P23; it does not any more. Only removing both extremes at once costs significance, and even then the direction holds.

I had the mechanism wrong, and the data says so

Last week I suggested players judge the timing more harshly. Broken out by item, that is not where the gradient lives:

Q5 noticeablerho −0.482p 0.023 4.71 / 4.14 / 4.00
Q6 fittedrho −0.427p 0.047 4.57 / 4.14 / 3.00
Q7 synchronisedrho −0.285p 0.199 4.50 / 4.14 / 3.00

It is carried by noticeability and fit; synchronisation alone does not reach significance. So the reading is broader than timing: people who play are harsher about whether the haptic matched the game at all. The timing dissents (P23’s “slightly off”, P12 anticipating the pulse “before every hitting”) are consistent with it but are not what produces the statistic.

4Two things that could still explain it away

1. Playing is entangled with gender here. Six of the eight who have played are women; nine of the fourteen who have not are men. Since women also rate system quality slightly lower (4.21 against 4.58), part of the tennis contrast could be a gender contrast. It does survive splitting: within women rho = −0.592 (p = 0.055), within men rho = −0.310 (p = 0.354), and the played/never gap appears in both rows of the 2×2 — women 3.94 against 4.53, men 4.33 against 4.63. But with two male players that separation is weak. The cheapest fix is recruiting male players.

2. “Regularly” is one person. The bottom of the gradient is P12 alone. Removing them keeps the result (p = 0.034), so it is not load-bearing, but the shape of the curve past “occasionally” is a single observation.

Multiplicity still applies: this is one of eight comparisons and a Bonferroni correction for eight would require p < 0.006. What earns this one more credit than the others is that it was a directional prediction made before these two participants, not a pattern found by looking.

5What I would say in one sentence each

Gender: still nothing, now on equal groups of 11 and 11 — 0.12 apart on experience, p = 1.000. The one caveat is that gender is tangled with playing, so it should not be dismissed until the player group is less female-heavy.

Order: +0.55 on experience for seeing vibration first, p = 0.186, and now on perfectly balanced groups so the imbalance excuse is gone. It remains confounded with clip content, which is the one thing a repeat can fix for free by recording which clip carried the haptics.

Tennis experience: the result of the study so far. More playing, lower system rating, monotonically, p = 0.011, robust to dropping either extreme. It is about whether the haptic matched the game, not specifically about timing. Next step is recruiting players — especially male players — because that both strengthens the estimate and unpicks the gender confound at the same time.
