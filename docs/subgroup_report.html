Three questions from last week: does the viewing order, tennis experience, or gender explain how people responded? Short answer: gender no, order probably not, tennis experience maybe — and none of it reaches significance at this sample size.

1Who took part

Twenty participants, recruited by convenience between 14 August and 11 September. The sample is young-to-middle-aged, evenly split by gender, and light on tennis: nobody watches it more than monthly and fourteen have never played.

2What “the result” means here

Each participant gets two scores, each the mean of three items on the same 1–5 scale. System quality is noticeable + fitted + synchronised, and asks whether the detector worked: overall 4.47 (sd 0.70). Experience is enjoyed + engaging + would-install, and asks what they made of it: overall 3.55 (sd 1.29). Every comparison below is against these two.

The tennis-experience result rests heavily on one person

Six participants play tennis occasionally, and the contrast is worth showing only with its sensitivity attached. Dropping each player in turn and recomputing the system-quality difference:

all 20diff +0.43p 0.073
drop P03diff +0.53p 0.051
drop P06diff +0.53p 0.051
drop P13diff +0.46p 0.102
drop P20diff +0.46p 0.102
drop P17diff +0.40p 0.136
drop P23diff +0.20p 0.177

Removing P23 more than halves the effect. P23 plays tennis, rated synchronisation 2, and is the only participant to dispute the timing in writing (“slightly off”). So the finding is partly a restatement of that one dissent.

The direction survives regardless — every leave-one-out is still positive, and players average 4.40 against 4.60 even without P23 — but the magnitude does not. The hypothesis worth testing, not claiming: people who have played tennis have a bodily reference for what a struck ball feels like, so they judge the timing more harshly. Note the direction is a double one: players rate the system lower (4.17 vs 4.60) while rating the experience higher (3.89 vs 3.40). They are more critical and more engaged at the same time.

4Why none of this is a result yet

Four reasons, in order of how much they matter.

1. Nothing is significant. The smallest p-value is 0.073. I ran eight comparisons (two outcomes × four background variables with usable group sizes); a Bonferroni correction for eight tests would require p < 0.006. Nothing is close.

2. The groups are tiny. Six players, nine women, nine no-vibration-first. A difference of 0.4 on a three-item mean is one participant changing two ticks.

3. Order is confounded with clip content. The haptic and non-haptic clips were different footage and which clip carried the haptics was not recorded, so the +0.53 order difference may be partly a statement about which rally was more interesting. This is the one confound a repeat can remove for free, by recording one extra field.

4. Age, haptics familiarity and phone habit could not be tested at all — each splits into three or more groups with cells of 1 and 2. They are reported as descriptives only. For what it is worth, the largest raw gaps there are experience by age (18–24 at 4.11 against 35–44 at 3.27) and by phone habit (never in hand, 2.89, n = 3).

5What I would say in one sentence each

Gender: no relationship, and this is the cleanest null in the data — the two groups differ by 0.06 on experience, and by 0.17 with the four flagged sheets removed. If a supervisor asks whether to recruit for gender balance, the answer from these twenty is that it will not change the outcome.

Order: seeing vibration first is associated with a half-point higher experience score, which is the largest experience gap in the study and is not significant. It survives removing the four flagged sheets (+0.63, p = 0.341), so it is not an artefact of those. It is confounded with clip content, so I would not report it as an order effect until the assignment is recorded.

Tennis experience: the only contrast with a large effect size, and the only one I would spend more participants on. Six players is too few, and one of them is doing much of the work. Recruiting players specifically is the cheapest way to find out.
