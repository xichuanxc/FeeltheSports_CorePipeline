Twenty-Two Sheets, One Dissent

Chuan Xi · COMPX520
University of Waikato
N = 22 · 14 Aug – 17 Sep 2026

Twenty-two people watched two tennis clips, one with smartphone haptic feedback driven by the acoustic detector. For twenty-one of them the buzzes landed in the right place. One disagreed — and wrote down why. Whether they wanted the buzzes at all is where the group came apart, and four sheets disagree with themselves on exactly one tick each.

The dissent, and why it is worth more than one row of data

P23 answered disagree on “the vibrations feel synchronised in time with the match events” and, unprompted, wrote two words in the margin beside the tick — the only marginal annotation in twenty-two sheets:

“slightly off”

That is a diagnosis, not a complaint, and it matches the direction of the measured error. Firing runs a median 33 ms early against hand-labelled onsets, so anyone who notices at all should notice the buzz arriving slightly before the strike. P02 independently described “a slight mismatch in the timing”. Neither was told what to look for.

Twenty of twenty-two noticed nothing, so 33 ms sits below threshold for most people and not for all — the expected shape for a tolerance that varies between individuals, rather than evidence the timing is wrong. P23 is also the profile most likely to know what a struck ball should feel like: very familiar with phone haptics, watches tennis, plays occasionally. P12, the first participant who plays regularly, answered neutral on synchronisation and described anticipating the pulse — “before every hitting. I became kind of thinking the feedback” — which is what an early cue feels like to someone who knows the rhythm of a rally. The cheapest test is to delay the fire point by ~30 ms. That is a constant in the playback path, not a model change.

2What that means for the detector

The timelines participants felt came from the conventional acoustic front end alone — no classifier was involved. On that pipeline, measured recall was 69.4% and precision 72.6%, at 4.47 false buzzes per minute. Viewers still rated synchronisation 4.32 of 5, and not one mentioned a spurious vibration. The remaining barrier is not detection accuracy but information, attention and physical cost. Seven of twenty-two asked the channel to say which hit rather than merely that a hit occurred — P23 wanted it to fire “only with point-winning hits” — and of the five lowest experience scores, three are that objection rather than a complaint about accuracy. P19, who rated synchronisation 5, reported a tingling that outlasted the clip and answered not sure on installing it for that reason alone.

What I think this is, and what I would change

Two of the four sit on the only two items that break the pattern of the battery: Q8 is the sole reverse-worded statement, and Q10 the sole item not on an agree/disagree scale. The other two are both on Q3, the first Likert row on the page, and both land at the extreme opposite end from everything else that participant wrote.

Four contradictions in twenty-two sheets is a rate worth designing against rather than attributing to four careless people. For the next round: word every item in the same direction and measure distraction by its absence, keep one scale for the whole battery, and add a single confirmation question at the end that restates the two answers most likely to be mis-ticked.

None of this touches the principal finding. All four rated the system items 4 or 5; the contradictions fall on the experience side, which is the half already known to be divided. The one sheet that does touch the principal finding — P23 on synchronisation — is not a contradiction at all. It is internally consistent, negative throughout, and argued.
